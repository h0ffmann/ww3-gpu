# THEME

WAVEWATCH III® (WW3) is a numerical model for forecasting ocean waves, developed by the National Centers for Environmental Prediction (NCEP) of the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) and distributed as open source [@ww3manual; @ww3repo]. The model represents the ocean as a grid of points and describes the sea state at each point by the wave spectrum, the distribution of wave energy over frequencies and directions. At every time step this energy is propagated across the grid and modified by the so-called source terms: energy gained from the wind, energy exchanged among the waves themselves, and energy lost to wave breaking. WW3 belongs to the class of third-generation models, which solve the wave action balance equation (action being energy divided by frequency) without imposing the shape of the spectrum in advance [@wamdi1988; @tolman1991]. The code is written in Fortran and parallelised with MPI and OpenMP, and a *switch file* selects at compile time which parts of the code enter the executable, such as the physics, the propagation scheme and the I/O options [@ww3manual]. In Brazil, the Laboratório de Engenharia e Ciências Oceânicas of the Universidade Federal de Santa Catarina (LabECO/UFSC) runs WW3 in its operational forecasting system, as part of the Rede Nacional de Observação e Monitoramento Oceânico (ReNOMO) [@renomo; @labeco].

The theme of this project is the **parallel simulation and GPU acceleration of a spectral wave model**, taking WW3 in LabECO's operational configuration as the case study. The question behind the work is how much of the wall-clock time of a forecast cycle can be cut without the forecast fields moving away from the current ones beyond tolerances agreed with the laboratory, and whether a GPU helps when the model state stays in CPU memory. The work starts from two hypotheses, both testable by measurement. The first is that the current installation still has run time to gain from build and configuration changes alone. The second is that, even with the model state kept on the CPU, running the most expensive routines on a GPU reduces the total cycle time, despite the cost of moving data between CPU and GPU at every time step.
